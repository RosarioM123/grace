Grace AI prototype

Type how a teenager might actually write on a bad night, or a good one. The prototype reads the words, checks for crisis language before anything else, scores the feeling, checks the drafted reply before showing it, and writes one entry in a log. Every word that moved the score shows up in the readout under the box, with its weight next to it. Nothing is hidden.

Technical brief

TheGirlSavedByGrace is a faith-based mental health platform for teenagers and Gen Z. Rosario founded it in January 2020, when she was 14, after her own stretch of depression, anxiety, and loneliness, and after her Christian faith carried her through it. More than 200,000 teenagers in 100 countries have come through the platform since then.

The need is not abstract. 47% of Gen Z report mental health challenges, and youth suicide rose 62% from 2007 to 2021. Plenty of teenagers will type how they feel into a phone long before they say any of it out loud. Grace wants to be worth typing into.

How I built this

I built this demo in an evening, as one HTML file, so it can be hosted anywhere and read top to bottom. The word list is hand-written: 140 words and 25 phrases, each with a weight I picked, plus 31 crisis phrases that are checked before any scoring happens. The samples are written the way teens actually type, lower case and half finished, and nothing on this page phones home, because there is no server behind it and nothing typed here is kept.

The word list

These are real entries from the list, with the weights the prototype actually uses:

hopeless -3.1, worthless -3.2, lonely -2.7, anxious -2.5, numb -2.5, alone -2.2, tired -1.4, okay +0.7, calm +2.0, hopeful +2.4, grateful +2.6, blessed +2.8

Trade-off: a list I can read and defend line by line, instead of a model I cannot inspect. I gave up accuracy to get that. A list misses sarcasm, misses context, and misses every word I did not think of. That is the honest reason the production system uses trained spaCy and NLTK models, and the reason this page calls itself a prototype.

The scoring code

This is the actual step that scores one word, copied from the script at the bottom of this page:

// score one word: start from its weight,
// then adjust for the word right before it
if (BOOSTERS[prev]) val = base * BOOSTERS[prev];
else if (DIMINISHERS[prev]) val = base * DIMINISHERS[prev];
if (NEGATORS[prev] || NEGATORS[prev2]) val = val * -0.74;
matches.push({ term: w, valence: val });
themes[theme] = (themes[theme] || 0) + Math.abs(val);

So "so tired" counts more than "tired", "kinda numb" counts less than "numb", and "not happy" flips sign instead of scoring as happy. The compound score is the sum of the matched weights, scaled: sum divided by the square root of sum squared plus 15, which keeps it between -1 and +1.

How the system would work

A teen writes in free text. There is no form to fill in and no score to game. Sentiment analysis reads the feeling as the words come in. If risk language shows up, the crisis protocol takes over and everything else waits: the teen sees 988 and the Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741) before any content, and the case moves toward a licensed professional with a same-day goal. When there is no crisis, the reply is picked from what the teen actually wrote. A testimony or video on the same theme. A verse or a prayer. One small coping step, sized to the day they are having. The demo at the top of this page runs that flow, with the word list standing in for the trained models.

The production plan: ReactJS for the front end, MySQL for storage, AWS EC2 to run it, spaCy and NLTK for the language work, and a matching step that connects a teen to a licensed mental health professional.

The safety layer

I run two separate checks, because one check has one way to fail and this system does not get to fail quietly. The first check reads the teen's message as it arrives and looks for risk language. The second reads the reply I drafted, before the teen sees it, and can block it. Both read against a written safety policy that names what counts as risk language and what a reply must do when it appears. A crisis flag overrides the personalized reply completely. Before a version ships, I run a set of crisis messages I wrote to try to break the first check. If it misses one, that version does not ship.

Trade-off: two checks cost time, and they will sometimes block a reply that was fine. I accept that. A blocked good reply is a small loss next to a bad reply reaching a teen at the wrong moment.

Failure mode: if the first check misses risk language anyway, the failure is contained two ways. The crisis numbers sit at the bottom of every page whether anything is flagged or not, and any run the scorer is unsure about is flagged for a person to read. A miss is bad. A miss with no backstop would be worse.

Before anything ships

I score five things separately before a release: sentiment accuracy, crisis recall, crisis false-positive rate, response safety, and latency. Each one has a threshold set ahead of time, with clinical reviewers in the room. One line over its threshold and the version does not ship, whatever the other four say. I have not set the threshold numbers yet, and I would rather leave the space open than invent them here.

Personalization follows the same discipline. A new way of picking content goes to a small group first and is measured against the current version on two things: whether teens stay with the reply, and whether teens who need a person actually reach one. If the numbers do not move, the change does not ship, however good it sounded in the meeting where I proposed it.

How matching works

I score a match in two parts, and I keep the parts separate so either one can be questioned. One side holds what the teen needs: what they are feeling, the language they use, faith preference, availability, urgency. The other side holds the professional: specialty, language, faith background, availability, verified credentials. The first part of the score is how close those two sides sit. The second part is trust, from verified credentials and how recent the verification is. A fast match to the wrong person is not a good match, so safety runs through this step instead of sitting at the end of it.

Trade-off: ranking on trust as well as closeness means the closest match on paper sometimes loses to a professional whose credentials I could verify more recently. I would rather be able to explain that choice than hide it inside one blended number.

Failure mode: if the match is wrong, the teen can ask for a different professional with one tap, and the request does not need a reason.

The log

Every interaction is written once, to a log that only appends. Every action carries a key generated for that action, so a retry sends nothing twice and matches nobody twice. Each entry holds a hash of the entry before it, so a later edit anywhere in the chain shows. I have built this shape before: WORLD, a system of mine, is a deterministic state engine on a SHA-256 hash-chained intent ledger, where every state change is checked against capacity, liquidity, and node-lock constraints before it commits, with a FastAPI dashboard for review. The Grace log reuses that chaining idea. The readout above writes an entry on each run and shows its key and its hashes, so the write-once behavior is visible here instead of just claimed.

Trade-off: an append-only log grows forever and makes deletion awkward, and deletion matters when the writer is a teenager. I keep the design anyway, with the retention question listed openly below, because a record I cannot quietly edit is the only kind worth trusting in a system like this.

What could go wrong

Missing a crisis message. The worst failure this system has. The mitigations are the two checks, the red-team set before any release, crisis numbers visible on every page regardless of flags, and unsure runs going to a person. None of that makes the risk zero. It makes a miss survivable instead of silent.

Matching a teen to the wrong professional. A bad match at a bad moment can close a teen off from help entirely. The trust part of the score, the one-tap change with no reason required, and crisis cases going to the front of the line are the mitigations I have. The weighting question underneath it is still open, and it is listed below rather than settled quietly here.

Teens not trusting an AI with their feelings. A fair worry, and the one I can do the least about with engineering. What I can do: show the score's work, every word and weight, the way the readout above does. Keep a person in the loop whenever the score is low or the reading is unsure. And in this demo, keep nothing that anyone types.

One more failure mode, smaller but real: if the sentiment score is simply wrong, the teen gets content for the wrong feeling. That is why every reply on this page carries a row of buttons to pick a closer feeling by hand. Try it above. The reply changes, and the run is still logged with the original score.

What I would build first, in order

This is also the order the $100,000 would be spent in. Each step comes where it does because the next one needs it.

  1. Crisis detection, as two trained checks. First because nothing else is allowed to reach a teen before this is live.
  2. Sentiment analysis on trained spaCy and NLTK models. Second because the reply and the match both key off the feeling it reads, and the hand-written list is the weakest part of the current design.
  3. The log, in production form. Third because matching writes records that must never duplicate, and that guarantee has to exist before real matches happen.
  4. Matching to licensed professionals. Last of the four because it needs the feeling, the safety checks, and the log all working first. Done means a teen in crisis can reach a professional the same day.
  5. Outcome tracking, and the small-group tests. Measuring whether replies helped only means something once the rest is running. Done means outcomes are followed across the network, not just visits counted, and more testimonies and professionals get added on evidence.

Open questions

  • Whether the crisis check should read the whole conversation or only the latest message. Reading everything catches a slow build. It also raises the false alarm rate. I have not decided.
  • Whether faith background should weigh more or less than specialty in a match. I will test both orders on the small group before choosing, instead of choosing now on instinct.
  • How long the log should keep a teen's words. Safety argues for keeping them. Trust argues for deleting them sooner. This one needs a clinical reviewer and a lawyer in the room, not just me.
